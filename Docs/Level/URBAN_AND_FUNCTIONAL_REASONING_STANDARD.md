# Urban and Functional Reasoning Standard

**Status:** Adopted; amended through `SOS-GOV-AAPEC-V1` on 2026-07-29.

## Purpose

This standard makes environment placement follow causes rather than decoration. It applies to urban, suburban, rural, interior, natural, historical, stylised, and fictional spaces by adapting the same chain:

`intended use -> users and access -> required function -> visible trace -> gameplay adaptation`

It does not require a complete or literal real-world simulation.

## Placement Rules

| Subject | Required causal reasoning | Common fail |
|---|---|---|
| Waste and disposal | Users, waste source, collection route, enclosure, clearance, and maintenance state explain the location. | Decorative bins or dumpsters with no reachable service path |
| Deliveries and loading | Supplier approach, vehicle/porter access, service surface, transfer point, storage, and conflict with public movement are resolved. | Loading door or bay that cannot be reached or used |
| Parking and stopping | Capacity and location follow adjacent uses, turnover, users, time pattern, and street/access constraints. | Parking placed only to fill empty space |
| Building access | Public, staff, resident, emergency, and service entries match the building's function and circulation. | Frontage, back, and interior use contradict each other |
| Facade and roof technology | Vents, ducts, meters, extraction, drainage, power, communications, and safety equipment follow plausible internal functions and routes. | Technical props scattered without a served system |
| Pedestrian and vehicle flow | Entrances, desire lines, crossings, grades, turning, stopping, conflict points, and recovery space support actual users and gameplay modes. | Paths that terminate arbitrarily or force accidental traps |
| Wear, dirt, and repair | Traffic, touch, load, leaks, weather, maintenance, impact, and age determine location, direction, intensity, and layering. | Uniform grime, random damage, or wear in unused areas |
| Active, closed, and abandoned use | Access, security, lighting, upkeep, inventory, signage, damage, and temporary reuse make states distinguishable. | State exists only in documentation and is unreadable in play |
| Public fronts and service backs | Presentation, security, deliveries, waste, utilities, staff movement, and privacy produce different but connected conditions. | Every side treated as the same decorative facade |
| Boundaries and map edges | Topography, construction, security, water, infrastructure, destination logic, framing, or authored continuation explains why play ends. | Void, arbitrary wall, blocked street, or obvious kit seam |
| Natural or fictional systems | Terrain, water, climate, ecology, resource flow, technology, ritual, or approved world rules provide the equivalent cause. | Urban rules copied literally into a different setting |

## Purpose and Host Requirement

Every visible element **MUST** have at least one defensible gameplay, world,
art-direction, or composition purpose. Every retained or new visible prop
**MUST** also have a credible spatial, architectural, functional, or gameplay
host. Placement used only to fill empty space **MUST NOT** pass.

The applicable functional plan or Task-Delta Work Order **MUST** record for
each prop:

- exact host;
- purpose;
- orientation;
- required pedestrian, vehicle, entrance, service, maintenance,
  accessibility, interaction, and camera clearance; and
- why a plausible user, resident, customer, worker, service operator, or
  gameplay actor would place or use it there.

If the host or purpose cannot be defended, the default decision is `REMOVE` or
`DO NOT PLACE`. A nearby wall, surface, route, or empty area is not a host
unless its function supports the prop.

Typical host relationships include seating in an intentional waiting, social,
rest, or frontage zone; waste at a reachable collection point; pallets at a
loading, delivery, warehouse, or workshop zone; awnings at real openings;
utilities at served walls or technical zones; gates at credible bounded
access; bollards at a protection or access-control condition; and static
vehicles in parking, loading, service, or ownership contexts. These are
examples, not project-independent asset prescriptions.

## Real-Use Clearance Rules

Placement **MUST** preserve pedestrian and vehicle circulation, entrances and
exits, loading, service and maintenance access, accessibility, practical use,
visibility, collection logic, and required spatial clearance.

Work **MUST NOT**:

- block a door, route, entrance, exit, service point, or required access;
- place seating or furniture directly in an active movement path;
- distribute furniture or service props randomly;
- place loading equipment without a loading or transfer context;
- run curb or boundary geometry across a valid entrance;
- retain duplicated, coplanar, overlapping, or visibly z-fighting surfaces;
  or
- accept a placement that is plausible only from one review angle.

## Decision Test

For each major zone or placement, answer:

1. Which user, activity, system, or gameplay need causes it?
2. How is it reached, operated, maintained, supplied, or abandoned?
3. What visible traces should that cause produce?
4. What must remain clear or usable for gameplay and camera?
5. What has been simplified or exaggerated, and why does that improve play?

If answers 1–3 are weak, remove, relocate, or redesign the element. If answer 4 conflicts with literal realism, gameplay may win under a documented adaptation. If answer 5 changes structure, record it in the decision log.

## Density and Detail Rule

Detail is required only where it explains use, supports gameplay, establishes hierarchy, or communicates approved mood/story. Quiet space is valid when intentional. Prop count is never a substitute for functional completeness or composition.

## Evidence and Gate

Use an annotated functional plan plus concise causal notes. At minimum show major zones, users, public/service access, foot and vehicle circulation where applicable, infrastructure, maintenance states, boundary causes, and gameplay adaptations.

The Level Designer owns the functional-planning decision. Art Director, Environmental Storytelling / World Design responsibility, and applicable specialists review their domains. A causal plan fails when a major zone has no plausible use or gameplay cause, a required service cannot operate, states are visually indistinguishable, a boundary is unexplained, or literal realism materially harms approved gameplay without an adaptation.
